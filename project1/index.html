CS 180 · Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire

The goal of this project was to reconstruct color photographs from Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii's digitized glass plate collection from the early 1900s. Each scan contains three grayscale exposures taken through blue, green, and red filters, stacked vertically in BGR order.

Single-Scale Alignment

The first approach I took to aligning the images was an exhaustive search over possible displacements within a [-15, 15] pixel window. I shifted the red and green channels relative to the blue channel, then scored each displacement using the L2 norm and normalized cross-correlation (NCC).

L2 distance

L2(A, B) = sqrt(sum((A - B)^2))

A smaller L2 distance represents a better match. In my implementation, I negate the value so both metrics can use the same maximization procedure.

Normalized cross-correlation

NCC(A, B) = ((A - mean(A)) · (B - mean(B))) / (||A - mean(A)|| ||B - mean(B)||)

A larger NCC score represents a better match. Normalizing the images makes NCC less sensitive to overall brightness differences between the color channels.

Ignoring the Borders

The borders of the glass plate had a large impact on the scores even though they were not part of the actual image. To reduce this effect, I computed the alignment score using only the centered 80% of each image. This crop was used only for comparison, with the final output still using the complete image.

Multi-Scale Image Pyramid

An exhaustive search works with small jpeg images, but becomes much more expensive for full resolution TIF files. The images are much larger, so some of the channels need to be displaced by well over 100 pixels. Searching the entire possible displacement range directly at full resolution would require evaluating far too many candidate alignments.

To make this more efficient, I implemented a recursive image pyramid. I repeatedly downsample both channels by a factor of two until the image is small enough to align efficiently. I first estimate the displacement at the coarsest resolution. Then, when I scale to the next resolution, I double the displacement because every pixel at the smaller scale corresponds to approximately two pixels at the larger scale. I then perform another search around that estimate to further refine the alignment. This process repeats until reaching the original resolution, allowing for large full-resolution displacements to be estimated using relatively small searches at each scale.

Offsets are listed as (dy, dx). Green and red were aligned relative to blue. NCC was used as the distance function.

Failure Case: Emir of Bukhara

The Emir image was the only one that did not align correctly when the red and green channels were aligned relative to blue. Parts of the image, especially the robe, have very different intensities across the three channels, so the raw NCC and L2 scores did not correspond to the correct geometric alignment.

To fix this, I aligned the red and blue channels relative to green. The red and green channels were more similar in this image, which produced a much better result.
